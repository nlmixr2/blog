The talk has seven parts. First, the dosing question. Second, what a pharmacometric model is. Third, the sparse data we have. Fourth, building the model in nlmixr2. Fifth, checking whether we can believe it. Sixth, using it to compare a flat dose with a weight-based dose. And last, how this fits into the wider R ecosystem.

1. The dosing question

Let’s start with the question.

A flat dose tuned for a 1.5 kg baby may not suit a 900 g baby

Preterm infants are prone to seizures, and phenobarbital is used to treat them. The dose makes the poison: too little and the seizures continue, too much and the baby can stop breathing.

A doctor read that one flat dose might work for every neonate. That would make giving the drug much simpler. But a flat dose tuned for an average one-and-a-half kilogram baby might not suit our nine-hundred-gram baby, and we don’t know where that baby would land.

Measuring each baby’s level cannot answer it: a preterm infant has less than a teacup of blood

The usual way to check exposure is to take blood samples and measure the drug level. But a preterm baby has less than a teacup of blood in its whole body, so a study with fifteen samples per baby isn’t possible.

What we have is scraps: one sample here, another three days later. So we need a model that learns how weight affects drug clearance from earlier data, and then simulates the dose instead.

2. What a pharmacometric model is

So, what is a pharmacometric model?

A pharmacometric model is a mixed model whose mean function is an ODE

If you’ve fit a mixed model before, you’ve done half of this already. The difference is the mean function. It isn’t a line or a spline. It’s the solution to a differential equation.

Think of a bucket with a hole in it. Drug goes in with the dose. The volume is how widely the drug spreads, and clearance is how fast it drains out. In nlmixr2 that bucket is one line of O-D-E. Each infant gets their own clearance: the typical value plus their own random deviation.

Volume sets the loading dose; clearance sets the maintenance dose

Models like this don’t just fit a curve. Their parameters mean something. The loading dose, the first big dose, is set by volume. It’s how much drug it takes to fill the bucket. The maintenance doses are set by clearance. They replace what drains out.

Because the model separates the two, it can design a whole dosing regimen. It can also predict doses nobody in the study actually got. That’s why drug labels and pediatric doses are so often built from models like this.

3. Scraps of data

Now let’s look at the data.

59 infants received 589 doses but gave only 155 concentrations

The data are fifty-nine preterm infants given phenobarbital, published in 19 85. They ship with nlmixr2 as pheno underscore S-D.

Each row in this plot is one infant, sorted by weight. The small ticks are doses and the circles are measured concentrations. There are five hundred and eighty-nine doses but only one hundred and fifty-five measurements. The babies were dosed often and sampled rarely, during routine care.

Forty percent of infants contributed two samples or fewer

Look closer and it gets worse. Nine infants have exactly one concentration. Forty percent have two or fewer. You can’t estimate two parameters for a baby from one data point. Unless, that is, you let the infants borrow strength from each other. That’s exactly what the mixed-effects model does.

4. Building the model in nlmixr2

Let’s build the model in nlmixr2.

An nlmixr2 model is a plain R function: ini() for estimates, model() for the ODE

pheno <- function() {
  ini({
    tcl <- log(0.008)   # log typical clearance (L/h)
    tv  <- log(0.6)     # log typical volume (L)
    eta.cl ~ 1          # between-infant variability on clearance
    eta.v  ~ 1          # between-infant variability on volume
    prop.sd <- 0.1      # proportional residual error
  })
  model({
    cl <- exp(tcl + eta.cl)
    v  <- exp(tv  + eta.v)
    d/dt(central) <- -(cl / v) * central
    cp <- central / v
    cp ~ prop(prop.sd)
  })
}

fit1 <- nlmixr2(pheno, pheno_sd, est = "saem")

The model is an ordinary R function with two blocks. In the ini block, an arrow sets a fixed effect and a wavy single line sets a random effect. The model block holds the differential equation, written the way you’d write it on paper. The last line connects the prediction to the observed data with a proportional error.

Notice there’s no dosing in the model. Doses come from the data. The rest is plain R, and one call to nlmixr2 fits it.

Adding body weight removes more than half of the between-infant variability

The base model gives thirty-seven percent between-infant variability on clearance, and forty-seven percent on volume. That means two infants on the same dose can easily differ two-fold in exposure. That’s why a flat dose is risky.

These babies range from point six to three point six kilograms. When we add body weight with standard allometric scaling, variability falls to about seventeen and eighteen percent. The objective function drops by about one hundred and forty-four points. More than half of the variability is just body weight.

Model piping adds a covariate without rewriting the model

phenoWt <- pheno |>
  model(cl <- exp(tcl + eta.cl + cov.cl.wt * log(WT / 1.3))) |>
  model(v  <- exp(tv  + eta.v  + cov.v.wt  * log(WT / 1.3))) |>
  ini(cov.cl.wt = 0.75, cov.v.wt = 1)

fit2 <- nlmixr2(phenoWt, pheno_sd, est = "saem")
# experimental in nlmixr2 7.0: let the estimator search for covariates
fit3 <- nlmixr2(pheno, pheno_sd, est = "vae")

We didn’t have to rewrite the model to add weight. With model piping, we swap the clearance line, swap the volume line, and set the two new initial estimates.

As an experiment in nlmixr2 seven point oh, we can also skip that step. The new variational autoencoder estimator searches for covariates by itself. Given the base model, it found weight too.

Left to search on its own, vae put weight back on both clearance and volume

Here’s what the vae search picked. On clearance, it found a power of weight with an exponent of about point six three, close to the textbook three quarters. On volume, it found a hockey stick that bends at one point three kilograms, steeper for the smallest babies.

It chose weight for both parameters, and volume variability dropped further, to fourteen percent.

5. Can we believe it?

Before we use this model for dosing, we need to check that we can believe it.

Infants with a single sample still get a full profile, borrowed from the population

First, let’s look at individual profiles. Infants fifty-six and twenty-eight gave one measurement each, and both still get a full concentration curve. Their individual and population curves nearly overlap. With one data point there’s nothing to pull the infant away from typical, and that’s the right behavior.

Infants two and eight have three samples each, and their curves separate. Now there’s enough information to say this baby isn’t typical.

Simulated studies bracket the observed percentiles, so the model is not misspecified

Second, a prediction-corrected visual predictive check. We simulate the study many times from the fitted model and ask whether the real data look like one of those draws.

The observed median and fifth and ninety-fifth percentiles, the solid and dashed lines, stay inside the simulated bands. With only one hundred and fifty-five observations the percentiles are noisy, but the model isn’t misspecified.

6. Flat versus weight-based dosing

Now we can answer the question we started with.

The simulation reuses the fitted model itself, so nothing is re-implemented

simOne <- function(w, flat) {
  ev <- et(amt = if (flat) 30  else 20   * w) |>  # loading
        et(amt = if (flat) 1.9 else 1.25 * w,      # maintenance
           ii = 12, until = 24 * 14, time = 12) |>
        et(seq(24 * 7, 24 * 14, by = 4))           # sample week 2
  rxSolve(fit3, ev, nSub = 400, iCov = data.frame(WT = w))
}

We compare two regimens. The flat one gives every infant a thirty milligram loading dose, then one point nine milligrams every twelve hours. That’s right for a one-and-a-half kilogram baby. The weight-based one gives twenty milligrams per kilogram to load, then one point two five per kilogram.

The simulation passes the fitted object straight to rxSolve. It samples four hundred new infants at each weight from the estimated variability. The model we simulate from is exactly the model we fit.

A flat dose is right only for the average infant; weight-based dosing stays in the window

Here’s the result. The gray band is the fifteen to forty milligram per liter window. The flat regimen, in red, hits the window at one and a half kilograms, because that’s what it was designed for. But it slopes down across the weight range: too high for the smallest babies, too low for the largest.

The weight-based regimen, in teal, stays flat and inside the window at every weight.

On the flat dose, fewer than 4% of 3.1 kg infants reach the window

Here are the numbers. At one and a half kilograms, the flat dose puts almost every infant in the window. At point seven kilograms, the median infant is at forty-one, above the window. At three point one kilograms, the median has dropped to twelve, and fewer than four percent of infants reach the window at all.

With weight-based dosing, ninety-six percent or more are in the window at every weight. And that weight was in the dataset all along.

This is a teaching example, not dosing guidance

  • A 1985 dataset, one compartment, one covariate
  • Real neonatal phenobarbital dosing also accounts for postnatal age and maturation
  • Dosing decisions belong to a qualified doctor and pharmacist
  • The workflow is the real one
  • Sparse data to model to simulation to a regimen decision

To be clear: this is a teaching example on a 19 85 dataset. We kept it to one compartment and one covariate on purpose. It is not dosing guidance. Real neonatal dosing also accounts for postnatal age and maturation, and it’s decided by a qualified doctor and pharmacist. The workflow, though, is the real one.

7. Where this sits in R

Finally, where does all this sit in R?

Nothing here left R, so there are no tool boundaries for errors to hide in

Nothing we did required leaving R. The fit object is a data frame, so every plot here is plain ggplot2 and every summary is plain dplyr. There’s no export step and no intermediate file format.

That’s not just convenient. Analyses usually break at the boundaries between tools, and this workflow doesn’t have any. Add renv to pin packages and targets to re-run only what changed, and someone else can rebuild the dosing recommendation from the raw data.

nlmixr2 connects to NONMEM and Monolix instead of walling them off

nlmixr2 isn’t a walled garden. That’s unusual in this field, and it may be the strongest practical reason to use it.

babelmixr2 runs the same model object through NONMEM or Monolix, so you can fit in nlmixr2 and confirm in NONMEM. It also connects to Pop Ed for optimal experimental design. Going the other way, nonmem2rx and monolix2rx bring finished runs into R, so years of legacy models can be simulated and checked with the same tools. Underneath it all, rxode2 solves both the fits and the simulations.

And there’s no license to negotiate, so any collaborator can run your analysis today.

Getting started is a smaller step than it looks. Everything in this talk is about forty lines of code. Install nlmixr2 from CRAN, and on Windows install Rtools too.

From there, the nlmixr2 website has the model language and worked examples. nlmixr2lib has starting models. If you’re coming from NONMEM, nonmem2rx will translate a model you already trust. And nlmixr2 seven point oh makes FOCEI solve in parallel.

Where this lands

  • 155 scattered concentrations were enough to estimate clearance and volume for 59 infants
  • Body weight explains more than half of the between-infant variability
  • A flat dose is right only for the infant it was designed for
  • The whole analysis, from data to regimen, is one R document
  • Weight-based dosing held 96% or more of simulated infants in the window at every weight
  • The same model runs in NONMEM and Monolix through babelmixr2

To sum up. One hundred and fifty-five scattered concentrations were enough to model fifty-nine infants. Body weight explains more than half of the variability between them. A flat dose is right only for the baby it was designed for, while weight-based dosing keeps nearly every simulated infant in the window. And the whole analysis is one R document that also connects to the rest of the field.

Whether your data come from sports analytics, supply chains or neonatal wards, the shape of the problem is the same: few observations per subject, a mechanistic model, and a decision someone has to make.
