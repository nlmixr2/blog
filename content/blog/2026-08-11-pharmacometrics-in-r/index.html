Preterm infants are highly susceptible to seizures primarily due to brain immaturity and an increased vulnerability to acute brain injuries and systemic imbalances, so if an infant is delivered pre-term they may be likely to have seizures.

So, with a preterm infant with seizures – what can we do in data analytics to support the right drug and dose the first time? As stated by Paracelsus, the dose makes the poison: Too little and the seizures continue, too much and the infant stops breathing. What information do we have – the birth weight (900 g) and the APGAR score.

To complicate the analysis, this was not created from a well controlled clinical study taking intensive (15+) blood samples from these pre-term infants. They only have less than a teacup of blood in their entire body! What exists is scraps of concentrations, one here another 3+ days later across only a few infants. All on different doses, measured with different people and labs.

Even with these limitations, we now know that the correct dose of a drug, phenobarbital, used to treat seizures in these infants. It is in the drug label, a dose given in mg/kg.

Getting a defensible dose out of that is what pharmacometrics does. It helps understand what the drug does to the body and what the body does to the drug. It helps understand the concentrations of doses in individuals and populations and helps pick the right drug for the population and in some instances for the individual.

This post works through the analysis of this dataset end to end in R with nlmixr2 – from 155 scattered measurements to a defensible dosing label while using an open-source tool for drug-development.

If you have fit a mixed model, you have done half of this

Drug exposure is a dynamic process. As a drug is being absorbed, it is also being distributed throughout the body, and cleared at the same time. Pharmacometric (PMX) models help us to address these dynamic processes acting together via ordinary differential equations (ODE). Think like a filling a bucket of water with a hole at the bottom - how fast the bucket fills depends on how fast the rate of water flows in vs flows out.

In pharmacometrics we understand, like other fields, we have different infant sizes, and different ways the drug flows, which means a method to solve differential equations, and characterize differences between individuals at the same time: the mean function inside the nonlinear mixed model that is not a line or a spline, it is the solution to a system of ODEs describing how drug moves through the body.

These movements and concentrations are characterized by two parameters the clearance (volume of blood eliminated of drug per hour) and volume of distribution (how widely the drug spreads through tissue), you can use these are physiological quantities to inform the mechanism of how the drug works:

  • They extrapolate. Once you know clearance, you know the concentration for a dose nobody in your study ever received.

  • They can describe meaningful relationships. Clearance scales with body size and kidney function; volume scales with body water. This has been studied by the field in models for quite awhile. Therefore, when you are using population level ways of better describing the data (ie covariates), you put them on where biology says they belong, not where it simply better describe the data.

  • They separate the parts of a regimen. The loading dose is governed by volume; the maintenance dose is governed by clearance. Estimating them separately is exactly what lets you design a regimen, and it is invisible if all you have is a fitted curve.

So the questions pharmacometrics answers are dosing questions: what dose, how often, by what route; who needs a different one (children, renal impairment, drug interactions, obesity etc); what happens when a dose is missed; how much exposure produces the effect and how much starts producing harm; and whether the Phase III trial is worth running at all, at which dose.

Dosing sections of drug labels are built from these models. Pediatric doses are routinely approved by adult model extrapolation rather than and unjustified pediatric trial.

In the clinic, these models help therapeutic drug monitoring – vancomycin (for MRSA infections), busulfan (for cancer), infliximab (Autoimmune/inflamitory) – where the need of the drug is great and individual variability of effect / toxicity needs to be carefully balanced in each individual. Both FDA and EMA have written guidance on population pharmacokinetics. It is a small field with an unusually direct path from a model object to what happens to a patient.

A first model

To describe the model you can use an ordinary R function with two blocks – an ini({}) and a model({}) block:

pheno <- function() {
  ini({
    tcl <- log(0.008)   # log typical clearance (L/h)
    tv  <- log(0.6)     # log typical volume (L)
    eta.cl ~ 1          # between-infant variability on clearance
    eta.v  ~ 1          # between-infant variability on volume
    prop.sd <- 0.1      # proportional residual error
  })
  model({
    cl <- exp(tcl + eta.cl)
    v  <- exp(tv  + eta.v)
    d/dt(central) <- -(cl / v) * central
    cp <- central / v
    cp ~ prop(prop.sd)
  })
}

Three things are worth pointing out, because they are the whole language:

  • ini({}) holds initial estimates. <- declares a fixed effect (and residual estimates) ~ declares a random effect with its initial variance.

  • model({}) is the model itself. d/dt(central) is a differential equation, declared in Lebneitz notation. This one says drug leaves the central compartment at a rate proportional to how much is there. Dosing is driven by the data (AMT/EVID), so doesn’t appears in this model code.

  • The last line, cp ~ prop(prop.sd), says the observed concentration is the predicted cp with proportional residual error. That is where the model meets dependent variable, DV.

Everything else is plain R. WT will be a column in the data; exp() is exp().

Fitting it:

# note here we use the := nlmixr2 save function to cache the fit
# since nonlinear mixed effects models can take some time to run.
fit1 := nlmixr2(pheno, pheno_sd, est = "saem",
                control = saemControl(print = 0, seed = 42),
                table = tableControl(cwres = TRUE, npde = TRUE))

You can print out the entire fit object, which is a modified data-frame of predictions and estimates together. Another way to look at the data is simply to look at the estimate:

Typical clearance is about r signif(exp(fit1$parFixedDf["tcl","Estimate"]), 3) L/h and typical volume about 1.42 L. But look at the last column: between-infant variability of 37% on clearance and 47% on volume. Two infants drawn at random from this population can easily differ two-fold in exposure on the same dose. That is the number that makes a single flat dose dangerous, and the next question is whether any of it is explainable.

One line, and half the variability disappears

These infants weigh between 0.6 and 3.6 kg – a six-fold range in body size. It would be strange if that did not matter. Larger bodies have larger livers and kidneys and more water to distribute drug into, and the standard way to encode that is allometric scaling: clearance scales with weight to the 3/4 power, volume scales linearly.

In general this covariate, and others, are there to explain why certain babies/individuals have more drug in their system than others.

The change to the model is one line each. In nlmixr2 instead of having to rewrite the whole model we can change pieces of the model with model piping:

phenoWt <-  pheno |>
  # first we modify the cl line
  model(cl <- exp(tcl + eta.cl + cov.cl.wt*log(WT/1.3))) |>
  # then the v line
  model(v  <- exp(tv  + eta.v  + cov.v.wt*log(WT/1.3))) |>
  # then we add the covariate effects to the ini block
  ini(cov.cl.wt=0.75, cov.v.wt=1)

fit2 := nlmixr2(phenoWt, pheno_sd, est = "saem",
                control = saemControl(print = 0, seed = 42),
                table = tableControl(cwres = TRUE, npde = TRUE))
tibble(
  model = c("Base", "+ allometric weight"),
  OBJF  = c(fit1$objf, fit2$objf),
  AIC   = c(AIC(fit1), AIC(fit2)),
  `BSV CL (CV%)` = c(fit1$parFixedDf["tcl", "BSV(CV%)"],

First, individual profiles. This is where the borrowing of strength becomes visible:

Answering the actual question

This analysis showed we have a model that says how clearance and volume change with body weight, so we can simulate regimens for infants across the weight range and ask which regimen actually keeps them in the therapeutic window of roughly 15-40 mg/L.

Two candidates. A flat regimen — one dose for every infant, chosen to be correct for the average 1.5 kg baby in this study. And a weight-based regimen in mg/kg. Both use a loading dose (which volume of distribution determines) followed by twice-daily maintenance (which clearance determines).

The simulation reuses the fitted object directly — rxSolve() takes fit3 and samples new infants from its estimated between-subject distribution:

set.seed(99)
wts <- seq(0.7, 3.5, by = 0.2)

simOne <- function(w, reg) {
  flat <- grepl("^Flat", reg)
  ev <- et(amt = if (flat) 30  else 20   * w) %>%                # loading
        et(amt = if (flat) 1.9 else 1.25 * w,                    # maintenance
           ii = 12, until = 24 * 14, time = 12) %>%
        et(seq(24 * 7, 24 * 14, by = 4))                         # sample week 2
  rxSolve(fit3, ev, nSub = 400, iCov = data.frame(WT = w),
          addDosing = FALSE) %>%
    as.data.frame() %>% mutate(WT = w, regimen = reg)
}

grid <- expand.grid(
  WT = wts,
  regimen = c("Flat: 30 mg load + 1.9 mg q12h",
              "Weight-based: 20 mg/kg load + 1.25 mg/kg q12h"),
  stringsAsFactors = FALSE)

sim <- bind_rows(Map(simOne, grid$WT, grid$regimen))

sim %>%
  filter(round(WT, 1) %in% c(0.7, 1.5, 2.3, 3.1)) %>%
  group_by(regimen, WT) %>%
  summarise(median = median(cp),
            `% in window` = 100 * mean(cp >= 15 & cp <= 40),
            .groups = "drop") %>%
  mutate(across(where(is.numeric), ~ round(.x, 1))) %>%
  arrange(regimen, WT)

The flat regimen was chosen to be right for the average infant, and it is: at 1.5 kg the median lands at 22 mg/L, comfortably mid-window, with virtually every infant in range. But it fails at both ends. The median 0.7 kg infant sits at 41 mg/L – above the top of the window entirely – while at 3.1 kg the median has fallen to 12 mg/L and fewer than 4% of infants reach a therapeutic concentration at all. That is a three-and-a-half-fold exposure gradient across the population, driven entirely by a covariate that was sitting in the dataset the whole time.

The weight-based regimen holds the median between 19 and 25 mg/L across the entire weight range, with 96% or more of simulated infants inside the window at every weight.

There is a subtlety worth noticing in that curve: the weight-based line still drifts gently upward with weight then plateaus.

A caveat, stated clearly: this is a teaching example on a 1985 dataset, deliberately kept to one compartment and one covariate. It is not dosing guidance. Real neonatal phenobarbital dosing accounts for postnatal age and maturation, and would be under the care of a qualified doctor and pharmacist. The workflow, though, is the real one. Where this sits in the R ecosystem

The part that is easy to miss in a walkthrough is that nothing above required leaving R. Many tools in pharmacometrics require a shuttle back and forth between 2 different tools and ecosystems.

The fit object is a modified tibble. as.data.frame(fit2) gives you observations, predictions, residuals, and empirical Bayes estimates in a tidy frame, which is why every plot in this post is plain ggplot2 and every summary is plain dplyr. There is no export step, no intermediate file format, no separate post-processing tool with its own data conventions. That sounds like a convenience and is really a reproducibility property: the places where analyses usually become irreproducible are the boundaries between tools, and this workflow does not have any.

That matters concretely. The whole thing — data prep, fit, diagnostics, simulation, report — is one Quarto or R Markdown document (this post is one).

One approach to keep this reproducible is to in the dependencies with renv, wire the fits into a targets pipeline so they only re-run when the model changes, and someone else can reproduce the dosing recommendation from the raw data with the R script or report.

The second thing worth knowing is that nlmixr2 is not a walled garden, which is unusual for this field and is probably the strongest practical argument for adopting it:

  • babelmixr2 takes the same model object you just read and runs it through NONMEM or Monolix. You are not choosing a camp — you can fit in nlmixr2 and confirm in NONMEM, which is a genuinely useful thing to be able to do when the model matters. It also connects to PKNCA for non-compartmental initial estimates and PopED for optimal design.

  • nonmem2rx and monolix2rx go the other direction, importing finished NONMEM and Monolix runs into R so that a decade of legacy models can be simulated, plotted, and checked with the same tooling.

  • rxode2 is the ODE solver underneath both fitting and simulation. The model used for the regimen comparison above is bit-for-bit the model that was fit — not a hand-re-implementation of it in a separate simulation tool, which is a classic source of quiet errors.

  • The surrounding ecosystem — xpose.nlmixr2 and ggPMX for standardized diagnostics, tidyvpc for VPCs, shinyMixR for run management, nlmixr2rpt for Word and PowerPoint reports — is described in more detail in an earlier post.

And then the ordinary open-source argument, which lands harder in pharma than people outside it expect: there is no license to negotiate. An academic collaborator, a CRO, a reviewer, or a curious statistician two floors away can run your analysis today.

Getting started

If you are a pharmacometrician who has been meaning to try this, the first model is a smaller step than it looks — everything above is about forty lines of code.

install.packages("nlmixr2")

That brings in the estimation engine, the ODE solver, and the plotting and diagnostic packages. On Windows you will also want Rtools, since models are compiled to C for speed. Then:

library(nlmixr2)
fit <- nlmixr2(pheno, pheno_sd, est = "saem",
               control = saemControl(print = 0))

Some places to go next:

  • The nlmixr2 website has the full model language reference and a gallery of worked examples.
  • nlmixr2lib is a library of starting models, so a two-compartment model with transit absorption is a lookup rather than a derivation.
  • If you are coming from NONMEM, nonmem2rx will translate a model you already trust, which is a considerably more comfortable way in than starting from a blank file.
  • nlmixr2 7.0, released this month, made focei solve in parallel across all OpenMP-enabled platforms, so the fits in this post are quicker than they would have been a year ago.

And if you are coming from outside the field entirely — from sports analytics, from supply chain, from anywhere people fit hierarchical models to messy observational data – the thing worth taking away is the shape of the problem. Fifty-nine subjects, most with one or two observations, a mechanistic model with parameters that mean something, and a decision at the end that someone has to make on Tuesday whether or not the analysis is finished.
